**Title**

Exposure-Adjusted Pedestrian Harm Is 16 Percent Higher per 10 Percentage Points of Neighborhood Poverty in New York City

**Authors**

Aditya Bakshi (affiliation: Cohere Labs Community)

**Abstract** (plain text, 1635 characters)

Pedestrian injury and fatality rates are often reported to be higher in lower-income neighborhoods, but rates per resident can confound differences in street hazard with differences in pedestrian exposure. We combine modeled pedestrian volumes for New York City with street centerline data and five years of police-reported crashes from 2019 to 2023. We infer crosswalks, group them into 41,937 crossing sites that are largely intersections, and assign crashes to the nearest site. Across 38,477 sites in tracts with complete data, pedestrian harm crashes rise 16% per 10 percentage point increase in the tract poverty rate (rate ratio [RR] 1.16, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.12 to 1.20), after accounting for crossing volume, street width, travel lanes, posted speed, tract commuting and land use, and borough. The ratio is 1.30 (1.26 to 1.35) when only crossing volume is held fixed. The estimate holds across estimators, site definitions, time periods, boroughs, alternative standard errors, and a truck route proxy. It falls to 1.10 within ZIP areas and rises to between 1.19 and 1.22 once sampling error in the poverty rate is corrected. Pedestrian deaths exhibit a similar gradient (RR 1.29, 1.17 to 1.42), resulting in part from more crashes and in part from a higher chance that a crash is fatal. Models that use residents alone as the exposure measure produce ratios of only 1.06 to 1.09, so the result is driven by the exposure adjustment rather than by the choice of geography. These are associations from observational data that lack vehicle traffic volumes. All code is open and rebuilds the numbers from public sources.
