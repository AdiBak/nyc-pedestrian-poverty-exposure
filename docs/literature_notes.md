# Literature notes

Notes from reading the sources cited in the related work section. Read on 28 October 2026.

## NYC Walks (Sevtsuk, Basu, Liu, Alhassan, Kollar)
Read the preprint, titled Spatial Distribution of Foot-traffic in New York City and Applications for Urban Planning. The published version is in Nature Cities, DOI 10.1038/s44284-025-00383-y, and its title may differ.
- Models peak-hour pedestrian volumes with the Urban Network Analysis framework, calibrated on NYC DOT counts from 2018 and 2019. 842 of 1,011 calibration counts were on crosswalks (83.3 percent), so the calibration leans toward intersections.
- Links crashes to LION nodes within 50 m, 2012 to 2023, and divides injuries by the sum of weekday AM, midday and PM volumes. Maps injuries per 1,000 pedestrians.
- States that the intersections with the highest injury risk are often outside Manhattan. No analysis by neighborhood income or poverty. Its equity discussion concerns street classification.
- Our assignment differs: 30 m of an inferred site, 2019 to 2023, six peak hours averaged.

## NYC Department of Health, Epi Data Brief No. 86, March 2017
- 497 pedestrian deaths, 2012 to 2014. Fatality rate per 100 miles of street by crash ZIP code poverty: 3.2 low, 6.9 medium, 6.1 high, 9.1 very high (Table 6, 484 deaths with a valid ZIP code). Poverty is the share below 100 percent of the federal poverty level, ACS 2010 to 2014.
- Denominator is street length, not exposure.

## Oregon DOT technical memo (Roll, 2021)
- Tract composite of poverty rate and share of people of color, in four concentration categories. Fatal and severe injury rate 35.7 in the highest against 12.8 in the lowest category. High-concentration tracts also have more arterial traffic, more transit stops and more walking and transit commuting.
- Descriptive. No regression or significance test. The memo cannot confirm that residents are the people injured. The text and Table 3 disagree on one pair of figures (203.8 and 54.4), so check before quoting more numbers.
- Report title and number need confirming from the source page.

## Minneapolis (Lindsey, Tao, Wang, Cao 2019, CTS 19-15; Tao, Lindsey, Cao, Wang 2021, JTLU 14(1))
- Safety performance functions with pedestrian and bicycle exposure (estimated from DPW counts at 173 intersections and 437 mid-blocks) and vehicle AADT, in log form. 2005 to 2017 crashes.
- Exposure improved fit and changed the top 1 percent high-risk locations. Pedestrian intersections: 42 percent of locations common to both model sets.
- Mean predicted pedestrian and bicycle crash risk at intersections was higher in areas of concentrated poverty (ACP50) than elsewhere, by t-tests.

## NYC Vision Zero library (second-hand summaries, check against the PDFs)
- Vision Zero Reimagined Action Plan 2026: killed or severely injured rate per 1,000 population fell about 14 percent citywide since 2019 and about 17 percent in Priority Investment Areas, which are defined largely by share of non-White and low-income residents. Rates are per resident. Pedestrian-specific figures by income are not given.
- Vision Zero Year 7 Report (2021): qualitative equity framing, no income breakdown of pedestrian harm.
- Pedestrian Safety and Older New Yorkers (2022): age-group rates per 100,000, no income analysis.
- Equity and Street Safety (NYC DOT, 2025) and the 2023 Borough Pedestrian Safety Action Plans Update could not be fetched. The first is the most relevant and should be read.
